Madam Chair, distinguished colleagues, the proposition for strict, immediate laws to regulate LLMs is not only premature but deeply counterproductive. To shackle this nascent, rapidly evolving technology with rigid, prescriptive legislation now would be to stifle innovation before we even fully grasp its immense potential for good.

Firstly, the technology is evolving at an unprecedented pace. Any "strict law" crafted today risks becoming obsolete by tomorrow, creating a regulatory labyrinth that paralyzes beneficial development rather than guiding it. Legislation is inherently slow; LLM development is extraordinarily fast. This mismatch will lead to ineffective governance and missed opportunities.

Secondly, many of the legitimate concerns raised – misinformation, fraud, and privacy violations – are already actionable under existing legal frameworks. We have laws against defamation, fraud, discrimination, and data misuse. The challenge lies not in a lack of laws, but in adapting and rigorously enforcing these frameworks to the *application* and *outcomes* of LLM deployment, not the underlying technology itself. We should hold *deployers* accountable for the *harmful uses* of LLMs, such as discriminatory hiring or financial deception, rather than attempting to regulate the complex, opaque internal workings of the models themselves.

Finally, overly strict, premature regulation risks ceding global leadership in AI. It will drive innovation, talent, and investment away, hindering our ability to harness LLMs for profound societal good – from medical discovery to educational advancement. Let us focus on agile governance, adaptive guidelines, and robust enforcement of *existing* harm-focused laws, allowing innovation to flourish responsibly, rather than strangling a nascent, transformative technology with a regulatory straightjacket designed for a static world.
